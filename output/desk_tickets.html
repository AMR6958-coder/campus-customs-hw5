Reflection · written after the full run of 2026-11-03

What running Campus Customs with five agents taught us

Every answer below is about this app and the three tickets it ran. The evidence is on this page: the Expected and Actual sections of the 101, 102 and 103 tabs, and the Cash tab. The rest of the record is in output/resolved_tickets.json, output/audit_trail.json and the working database.

The run in numbers
3 tickets, 9 passes by the Boss (2 + 4 + 3), 18 delegations, 190 tool calls, 314 timeline events, 211 audit records appended, 3 payments totalling $3,248, 4 drafts written and none sent
What went wrong
0 refused tool calls, 0 retries by the decision validator, 0 payments the human did not sign; 2 calls of "blocked" a manager would have called handled, fixed with two sentences in the Boss's prompt
What it cost
40 to 90 seconds and roughly 20 to 40 model requests per pass on gpt-6-luna; the one-tee order on ticket 101 used 82 tool calls across four passes

1. How we evaluate the agents on each ticket, and why

Before the team touched a ticket we wrote, on each ticket tab, what a good run would look like: who the Boss should call first, which delegations, which tools, and what the database should hold afterwards. After the run we filled the Actual section from the record, never from the agents' own summaries: the ticket timeline (every tool call with its arguments, every delegation and reply), the audit trail (every model step), and the tables themselves. Each ticket is then scored on five checks, in this order of importance:

  1. First call. Did the Boss send the ticket to the specialist whose remit it is: Facilities for the rent, Inventory for the tee and the hoodies? The first call is where a team wastes the most work when it is wrong, and it shows whether triage understood the ticket. All three passed (see "First call made" on each tab).
  2. Who worked it, and who stayed off it. The right specialists in, the wrong ones out, chains no deeper than boss → specialist → specialist. Facilities never appeared on 101 or 103; Inventory and Customer Service never appeared on 102. Every agent on a ticket it does not need is cost and noise, and every hand-off is a chance to lose context.
  3. Tools. The expected tools used, no forbidden ones (no agent ever saw pay or decide_approval), and no refusal that a prior check would have avoided. Tool calls are the only part of an agent's work that can be verified line by line, which is why the Actual sections list every one with its count.
  4. End state in the database after each pass. Status, approvals (kind, amount, who asked), payments, cash, drafts, orders, against the expected column. The database is what the business lives on and the only thing the agents cannot narrate their way around; after the first pass all three tickets matched exactly (the three approvals, two drafts, cash untouched).
  5. The final call is true. "Resolved" only if resolve_ticket was called, "waiting" only if an approval exists, and no claim that anything shipped or was sent. The back end enforces this with a validator because an earlier live run showed the Boss saying "resolved" without having resolved anything; in this run it fired zero times.

Two measures of cost sit beside the checks: passes and tool calls (2, 4 and 3 passes; 17, 82 and 91 tool calls), and the human's load (four decisions, nothing paid twice, nothing requested the balance could not cover, as the Cash tab shows). We score outcome and process together because either alone misleads: a ticket "resolved" by a false claim would pass an outcome test, and ticket 103's "blocked" after the approval was wrong on outcome but right on every fact ($264 against $152), which is a prompt problem, not an agent problem.

3. Is there a simpler option than one agent with tools? Yes, two, and this run shows when each is enough

Simpler than an agent at all: rules. Two of the three tickets followed a path a script could run without a model. Rent due within two days → request a rent approval for the lease amount (102). An overdue invoice that blocks a vendor → request an invoice approval, then a purchase order once it is paid (101). The MCP server already encodes the hard half of those rules as refusals (no shipping while an invoice is open, no payment below zero), so a rules layer for the predictable cases would move the same money with no variance and no model cost. What rules cannot do is 103: weigh a margin, a shortfall, two pending commitments and a customer's request into an offer, and write the note. That is where a model earns its place.

Simpler than our team: one agent with the same tools. Look at what the specialists actually did. On 102's re-work the Boss needed no colleague: it read three tools and resolved. On 101 and 103 the specialists mostly read tools and reported back, and each of them re-read the ticket on arrival (get_ticket four times by the Boss and five by Inventory on 101). A single agent with the twenty-seven tools and the same human approval gate would have done these three tickets with fewer calls, fewer passes and less to go wrong, because every hand-off in this run was where context got thin: in an earlier check, Accounting, asked for the rent, requested the invoice instead. What the team buys is real but not needed at this size: separate remits that can later get separate permissions, short prompts per role, a record that reads like a conversation ("Facilities confirmed", "Accounting requested"), and the ability to work tickets in parallel. Our verdict for a shop with three ticket types: start with one agent, the tools, and the human gate; add roles when one prompt grows too long to steer or when Accounting should see tools that Customer Service must not. The simplest correct system is the one with the fewest places to lose the thread.

4. Three new problems Campus Customs could hand this team today, with the tools already built

  1. The empty mug shelf. CC-MUG-CREST has been at 0 since the seed and no ticket asked about it. Elm City Gifts is the mug vendor with a three-day lead and no open invoice, and a mug costs $3.50, so forty mugs are $140, inside the $152 left on the Cash tab. The path is the one ticket 101 already walked: open_ticket (restock), Inventory's get_stock and list_vendors, create_purchase_order, Accounting's get_cash and request_approval, the human's pay, an expected date of 2026-09-03.
  2. The next rent notice, or a second lease. Lease 1 is now due 2026-10-02. When that notice lands, or if the shop takes a second space, it is ticket 102 again: Facilities with get_lease or list_leases, Accounting with request_approval for the rent, the human paying, the lease date moving. The only new question is cash, and the team already answered that kind of question on 103: with $152 against $2,400 it would say so and request nothing the balance cannot cover.
  3. A customer order that stock can fill or substitute. If Tauhid Zaman would take a medium (five on the shelf) rather than wait for September 5, or the AI Club would take its eight mediums plus larges (fourteen on the shelf), the team has everything it needs: get_stock and list_stock, get_pricing, a draft_customer_message with the alternatives, resolve_ticket. No money moves and no approval is needed; it is the 101 and 103 pattern without the vendor.

5. Three new problems the tools cannot resolve, and why

  1. Receiving the delivery and selling the goods. Purchase order 1 is ordered, but no tool can mark it received or put the tee on the shelf, and no tool records a sale or a deposit. The S shelf will read 0 forever and cash can only fall. That is why ticket 101 is resolved as "on order with a date" and not as "fulfilled": fulfilment lives outside this toolset, by the rule that cash only goes out.
  2. Changing a deal with anyone outside the shop. Asking Elm City Properties for a few days' grace (the "due in 2 days" notice on 102), a payment plan with Bulldog Print Co, a cheaper mug supplier: there is no send tool, no vendor or landlord channel, and the lease, invoice and vendor rows are read-only. The team can draft to a customer and ask the human for money; it cannot negotiate, and it cannot change a term.
  3. Money coming in, or new things. A refund, a customer deposit, a new product line, a price-list change: there is no deposit or refund tool and no tool that adds an inventory or pricing row. The cash squeeze on 103 ($264 needed, $152 left, $112 short) can be reported perfectly and relieved by nothing inside the system; only the human, outside it, can bring money in.

None of these gaps is an accident. The toolset was built to the data we were given and the rules we were set (no revenue, drafts only, money only out), and the agents behaved exactly as far as those tools reach and no further. That is the safer failure: a team that stops at the edge of what it can verify.

6. What else the run taught us

Facts came from tools; judgment had to come from us. Every number the agents used was read from a tool and every claim we checked against the database held. The team wavered only on what a word means: "done" for an order on back-order, "done" for a half-filled discount request, "due in 2 days" on a notice written before the rent was paid. Each is a convention a shop settles once; the model, lacking it, chose the stricter reading and said blocked. Two sentences in the Boss's prompt settled both for good, and no code changed.

Enforce in code what cannot be got wrong, prompt the rest, verify the seam. Money moved only through pay, against a named human's approval, never below zero, the amount read from the record; nothing sends email. Those rules were never tested by the models because they could not be broken. The prompts carried everything else and were cheap to adjust. Where the two meet, the Boss's final call, a validator checks the decision against the ticket; shaped behaviour is a guardrail that fires zero times.

The human kept the pen. Four decisions, each made with the agent's rationale word for word, the amount on the button, the ticket a link away, and every tool call behind the request one click down. Nothing was paid twice; nothing was requested that the balance could not cover; after each payment the Boss checked the records rather than trusting the toast.

Same prompt, different path. The re-work of ticket 102 ended resolved in one session and blocked in another on identical data, before the re-work was told what had just happened. Agents are not deterministic programs. What made that manageable was that every delegation, tool call and model step is on the timeline and in the audit trail, and that the expectations were written first, so "it seemed to work" became a checklist with named deviations.

Slower and chattier than a clerk, and no initiative. A pass took one to two minutes and dozens of tool calls, many re-reading what had not changed. For three tickets a day the record is worth more than the time; for three hundred it would need caching, fewer passes and a cheaper model for the specialists. And the mug shelf stayed empty because no ticket asked: agents did what they were asked and nothing more. A back office that wants initiative has to give standing orders.

7. What we would build next

A regression harness that replays the three tickets after any prompt change and scores the five checks in section 1 against the Expected sections automatically, so a fix for one ticket cannot quietly break another. A rules layer for the two predictable paths and one agent for the judgment calls, as section 3 argues. Least privilege and a credential per agent, so the audit trail names the actor at the database. Spend limits and a second approver above a threshold. A standing restock rule for empty shelves. And an agreed meaning of "done" written down before the agents start, because that, more than any model setting, decided how many passes each ticket took.

In one sentence

Treat agents the way a good manager treats new staff: a clear remit each, a small set of tools, a boss who delegates and makes the call, approvals above a threshold, a record of everything, and the expectation that the first week will surface a few conventions nobody thought to write down.
